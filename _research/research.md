# Research

My research focuses on understanding the formation and evolution of cosmic structure and extracting robust cosmological information from observations. I work at the intersection of galaxy clustering, galaxy formation, and cosmological inference, combining observational data, numerical simulations, and statistical modeling to connect the nonlinear distribution of galaxies to the fundamental physics of the Universe.

## Galaxy Clustering and the Galaxy–Halo Connection

Galaxies are biased tracers of the underlying matter distribution, and their clustering encodes information about both cosmology and galaxy formation. My research investigates how galaxies populate dark matter halos and how the resulting clustering can be used to constrain cosmological parameters. A particular focus is on small-scale galaxy clustering, the halo occupation distribution (HOD), and the physical assumptions underlying empirical models of the galaxy–halo connection.

As part of the Aemulus Project, I developed an emulator for galaxy correlation functions that enables efficient predictions of nonlinear galaxy clustering across cosmological parameter space. This work demonstrated the potential of small-scale clustering to provide competitive constraints on cosmology, including the growth of cosmic structure. I have subsequently explored the dependence of galaxy clustering on galaxy properties and examined the robustness of HOD-based inference when assembly bias and other empirical extensions are included.

More recently, I have investigated the interpretation of observed galaxy clustering using both semi-analytical models of galaxy formation and empirical HOD models. By comparing these approaches, my work aims to clarify which aspects of the galaxy–halo connection can be robustly constrained by clustering measurements, and which conclusions depend on modeling assumptions.

## Galaxy Surveys and the Modeling of Galaxy Populations

Interpreting galaxy surveys requires realistic predictions for the abundance, spatial distribution, and observable properties of galaxies. My research develops theoretical and simulation-based models of galaxy populations to understand the scientific potential of current and future redshift surveys.

I have used the semi-analytical model Galacticus and cosmological simulations to predict emission-line galaxy populations for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. These studies examine galaxy number densities, clustering, halo occupation, and the implications for measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations and redshift-space distortions. This work connects models of galaxy formation to survey design and helps quantify how uncertainties in galaxy populations affect cosmological forecasts.

More broadly, I am interested in building reliable forward models that connect physical galaxy populations to observable survey catalogs. Such models provide a foundation for interpreting data from next-generation spectroscopic surveys and for understanding the interplay between galaxy selection, clustering statistics, and cosmological information.

## Statistical Methods and Cosmological Inference

A central challenge in cosmology is to distinguish genuine physical deviations from statistical fluctuations and systematic uncertainties in observational data. My research develops and applies statistical methods to test cosmological models, constrain the expansion history of the Universe, and assess the reliability of cosmological conclusions.

My earlier work explored model-independent constraints on cosmology using distance measurements and developed approaches for evaluating cosmological models using observations of both cosmic expansion and structure growth. I have also investigated the use of Type Ia supernovae as cosmological probes beyond their conventional distance–redshift relation, including weak-lensing magnification and the effects of sample variance on distance measurements.

These studies address complementary aspects of cosmological inference: how to test theoretical models with minimal assumptions, how to exploit additional information in existing observations, and how to account for the finite and inhomogeneous distribution of cosmic structures. My goal is to develop inference methods that make efficient use of observational information while providing a careful assessment of statistical uncertainties and potential sources of bias.

## Future Directions

My research aims to establish a more robust connection between galaxy formation, nonlinear structure formation, and cosmological inference. By combining physically motivated models of galaxy populations with accurate simulation-based predictions and statistical methods, I seek to improve our understanding of the galaxy–halo connection and extract more reliable cosmological constraints from current and future surveys. In addition, my research also adopts new techniques and methodologies such as simulation-based inference, machine learning models and so on. The combination of these algorithms and the physical models can yield a better understanding of the universe we live in. And our latest works can be found from the publication page.
